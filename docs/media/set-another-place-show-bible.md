# SET ANOTHER PLACE
## Show Bible

---

## THE SHOW

**Title:** Set Another Place
*(I checked — no active podcast under that exact name. Verify the handle and trademark yourself before you print anything.)*

**Logline:**
> A woman travels the country with a knife roll and a folding table, cooks dinner in hostel kitchens for people she's never met, and asks them one question: who fed you growing up?

**The format in one line:** She cooks. They talk. Nobody performs.

**Why this works and most interview shows don't:**

Every interview podcast has the same problem — two people in chairs, facing each other, one performing curiosity and one performing candor. Everybody can feel it. The reason your show is different is structural, not stylistic: **you're not going to interview anyone. You're going to cook with them.**

People don't open up across a table. They open up *side by side*, hands busy, eyes on the cutting board, when talking is the second thing happening instead of the only thing. Any bartender, hairdresser, or long-haul driver knows this. Almost no podcaster uses it.

It also solves your specific constraint beautifully. You speak softly and rarely. A traditional host has to drive the conversation with energy you don't have and shouldn't fake. But a host who is *genuinely occupied* — chopping, plating, checking a pan — creates silence that other people rush to fill. **Your quiet is the interview technique.** Don't fight it. Build the whole show on it.

**The thesis underneath:** America has a loneliness problem that everyone diagnoses and nobody solves, because the proposed solutions are all apps and the actual solution is a table. You are going to demonstrate the fix, on camera, over and over, in the cheapest possible venue, until people start doing it themselves.

---

## THE SIGNATURE QUESTION

Every episode ends the same way. Same words, every time, once the food is gone.

> **"Who fed you growing up?"**

Not *what did your mom cook* — because plenty of people didn't have that, and the question would land like a slap. **Who fed you** works for everybody. The grandmother. The dad who only made three things. The older sister. The school cafeteria. Themselves, at nine, standing on a chair.

It gets past the resume in one move. Nobody has a rehearsed answer. And it will make people cry more often than you're ready for, which you should prepare for and never, ever push.

**This is also the Easter egg.** Your whole story is a woman who was taught to feed herself early. You'll ask this question a hundred times before anyone realizes you're also answering it.

## EPISODE ANATOMY

**Length:** 25–40 min video, 20–30 min audio cut. Resist going longer. The discipline is the product.

| Time | Beat | What's happening |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–1:30 | **The arrival** | You, alone, walking in with a bag. Voiceover: the city, the hostel, what you're making, why. Never say your own name. |
| 1:30–4:00 | **The ask** | Actual footage of you inviting strangers in the common room. Include the awkwardness. Include a no. The nos are the most valuable footage you will ever shoot. |
| 4:00–12:00 | **The prep** | Everyone cooks. This is where the real conversation happens. Shoulder to shoulder, hands moving, no eye contact required. |
| 12:00–14:00 | **The teach** | Two minutes, max. One technique, one swap, one thing they can do at home for cheap. This is the health pillar and it must never sound like a lecture. |
| 14:00–28:00 | **The table** | Everyone eats. You mostly listen. Let people talk over each other. Don't clean it up in the edit. |
| 28:00–32:00 | **The question** | "Who fed you growing up?" Around the table, one at a time. Cut nothing. |
| 32:00–end | **The dishes** | You washing up alone, or with one person who stayed. Quiet. One line of reflection. Recipe card on screen. |

**The rule that makes it a show instead of a vlog:** the food is the clock. Every episode is structured by the meal, not by topics. When the food's ready, the prep act ends. When plates are empty, the table act ends. You never have to decide what happens next — dinner decides. That's the same principle as your binder, applied to television.

## THE METHOD

**1. Hand everyone a job within ninety seconds.** Nobody sits and watches. A person holding a knife is a person who will tell you about their divorce. A person sitting with their hands empty is a person being interviewed.

**2. Never ask a question you could ask on a podcast.** No "so what do you do." Ask what's on the counter:
- "Do you cook at home?"
- "What's the last thing you made for someone else?"
- "What do you eat when nobody's watching?"
- "Are you good at this? Be honest."
- "When's the last time somebody cooked for you?"

That last one is the second-most powerful question in the show. Most people have to think about it, and the length of the pause tells you everything.

**3. Let silence run.** Count to five before filling it. Your instinct will be to rescue people. Don't. The five seconds after someone finishes a sentence is where the real sentence lives.

**4. Never correct anyone about food.** Someone will say something wrong about gluten or protein or veganism. Let it go. You are not there to be right — being right on camera is the fastest way to become someone people watch to dislike. Demonstrate, never argue.

**5. Cook something with a gap in it.** A dish with a step that requires two pairs of hands. Forced collaboration produces the footage.

**6. Feed people who are actually hungry.** Hostels are full of people eating badly because they're broke. Don't pretend this is a dinner party. It's not. That's the whole reason it's moving.

## WHAT YOU COOK

**Constraints that are actually creative advantages:** hostel kitchens have one dull knife, two mismatched pans, no oven half the time, and a hot plate. You will cook for 6–10 people for under $40. Everything gluten-free and plant-based, and — critically — **never announced as such.** People eat it, love it, and find out after.

That's your entire health strategy in one move. "I'm not preachy about it" isn't a personality trait here, it's a production rule.

**The rotation (three dishes, endlessly variable):**

1. **A big pot thing** — stew, curry, beans and greens. Scales infinitely, forgiving of bad equipment, smells incredible in a common room, which is itself a recruitment tool.
2. **A composed bowl** — grain, roasted vegetable, sauce, crunch. Everyone builds their own. Maximum participation, minimum skill required.
3. **Flatbread or fritters on the hot plate** — hands-on, fast, and there's something about people making bread together that dissolves a room.

**The teach segment rotates through:** how to make one sauce that fixes anything · how to shop a grocery store perimeter for $30 · why beans are the answer · seasoning by tasting instead of measuring · what to actually buy for a kitchen you don't own.

## PRODUCTION

### Gear, in the order you should buy it

**Tier 1 — start here, and it's genuinely enough:**
- Your phone. It's fine. It's better than fine.
- **Two wireless lav mics** (DJI Mic Mini or Rode Wireless Micro class). **This is the single most important purchase in this document.** Hostel common rooms are acoustic nightmares — hard floors, high ceilings, fridge hum, six conversations. Bad audio kills a show; mediocre video does not.
- A small tripod that grips a shelf or table edge.
- One cheap LED panel with a warm gel. Hostel kitchens are lit like interrogation rooms and it will make beautiful food look like a morgue.

**Tier 2 — once episodes are landing:**
- Second phone or a used mirrorless for a locked-off wide shot
- A third lav so table conversation is actually usable
- Your own knife, cutting board, and one good pan — travel with them, they become part of the visual brand

**The kit rule:** everything in one bag you can carry up hostel stairs. Your suitcase system already exists; this is a tab in it.

### Permissions — do not skip this

**Hostels:** you cannot walk in and film. Email management two weeks ahead. Most will say yes enthusiastically — you're giving them free content and a better guest experience — but some have policies and all of them have insurance. Ask specifically: can I film in the kitchen and common area, can I use your name and location, and can I put up a flyer.

**People:** you need a signed release from everyone identifiable on camera, especially if you ever monetize. Get a simple one-page release, keep it in your phone as a PDF, have people sign before the camera comes out — not after, when it feels like a trick. **This is not optional if you plan to run ads or take sponsorship,** and it protects them as much as you.

**The gentler version of the ask:** "I make a show where I cook dinner for strangers. You're welcome to eat either way — the food is happening regardless. If you'd rather not be on camera I'll seat you off-frame." Make the food unconditional. Everything changes when the meal isn't payment for participation.

### Safety — read this part twice

You're a woman traveling alone, filming strangers, publishing your location. That's not a reason not to do it. It's a reason to have a system, which is the thing you're best at.

- **Never post location in real time.** Publish a city only after you've left it. This costs you nothing and removes the largest risk in the whole project.
- **Hostel management always knows you're filming and when.** A staff member who knows your schedule is a safety net that costs one email.
- **One person always knows your itinerary** — dates, hostel names, check-in times. A standing text at the end of each shoot night.
- **Cook in common areas only.** Never in a private room, never a "let's continue this somewhere else."
- **You can end any shoot at any moment and owe nobody an explanation.** Write that down now, while it's abstract, so it's already decided when it isn't.
- **Food safety:** ask about allergies out loud before anyone eats. Every time. Say it on camera — it models the behavior and it protects you.

## ONE SHOOT, FIFTEEN ASSETS

Video podcasting is now the default, and YouTube has overtaken Spotify and Apple as the leading place people discover podcasts. Short clips are how anyone finds a show at all — listeners sample a stranger in 60 seconds before committing to 30 minutes. So the shoot isn't the product. The shoot is raw material for a distribution machine.

**From one hostel dinner:**

| Asset | Platform | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Full episode | YouTube | The anchor. |
| Audio cut | Spotify/Apple | Strip the visual-dependent bits. |
| **"Who fed you" clip** | Reels/Shorts/TikTok | Your recurring format. This is the one that goes wide. |
| The invitation clip | Reels | Including rejections. People love the nerve of it. |
| The teach clip | Reels | Genuine utility, highest save rate. |
| Recipe card | Instagram carousel | With affiliate links to ingredients. |
| 3–4 quote cards | Instagram | Straight from the table. |
| Substack essay | Substack | What you didn't put on camera. The real one. |
| Behind-the-scenes | Stories | The bag, the grocery run, the terrible kitchen. |
| Photo carousel | Instagram | Faces, hands, food, room. |
| Voice note | Substack | Two minutes, recorded that night, unedited. |

**The pattern:** publish clips continuously, not just on release day. One episode should feed two weeks of posting.

## BOOTS ON THE GROUND

The flyers matter more than you'd think, and here's why: a physical flyer in a hostel common room converts at a rate no algorithm touches, because the person reading it is *bored, in that building, tonight.* That's the highest-intent audience that will ever exist for this show.

**The flyer:**

> **FREE DINNER**
> Thursday · 7pm · the kitchen
>
> I'm cooking for whoever shows up.
> No cost. No catch. You can help or you can just eat.
>
> — Nova
>
> *(small, at the bottom: I film these. You can be off-camera. Food's yours either way.)*

Green paper. One frog in the corner. Hand-lettered, not designed — designed reads like a business, hand-lettered reads like a person.

**Where they go:** hostel common rooms and bunk doors, community boards at food co-ops, university housing, climbing gyms, laundromats, libraries.

**Word of mouth, engineered:** the last thing you do at every dinner is ask *"where should I go next, and who should I feed?"* People will name a city, a hostel, sometimes a person. Follow those. **A season routed by your guests' recommendations is a better story than a season routed by you** — and it means every episode ends with a thread pulling into the next one.

## SEASON ONE

**Ten episodes. One city per week, or every other week if you need to breathe.**

Route them by cost, not glamour. Hostels in cities with real hostel culture and cheap groceries: Asheville, New Orleans, Austin, Santa Fe, Portland, Chicago, Philadelphia, Savannah, San Diego, Minneapolis. Nobody has made this show in these cities. Everybody makes it in New York.

**Ep 1 must be local.** Shoot it where you live. Get every mistake out of the way somewhere you can sleep in your own bed.

**Ep 10 is the reunion.** Fly or drive back to the people who moved you most and cook for them again — this time in their homes. That's the season finale and it's the emotional payoff of the whole thesis: the table wasn't a one-time thing, it was the beginning of something.

**Release:** weekly, same day, same hour, forever. Your credibility is the schedule.

## HOW IT PAYS

**Now:** recipe cards with affiliate links · local restaurant and food-brand sponsorship in each city (a local brand will absolutely feed a table of ten travelers for tagged content) · Substack paid tier with full recipes and the city guides.

**Soon:** ticketed live dinners in cities where an episode did well — people who watched will pay to sit at the table · hostel and travel brand partnerships, which is an obvious fit almost nobody has claimed · cookware and pantry brands.

**Later:** this show is the single best possible marketing for Dig In, and not because you'll mention the app. Because you'll spend a hundred episodes proving that people are starving for a table and don't know how to build one. The app is the answer to the question the show spends a year asking.

**One honest note on cost:** this is the most expensive thing you've proposed. Travel, groceries, gear, and time. Fund episodes one through three yourself, locally and cheaply, before committing to a route. If three episodes don't move people, the format needs work — and you'll want to have found that out for a few hundred dollars instead of a few thousand.

---

## WHY THIS IS THE RIGHT IDEA

Everything you've told me across these plans has been circling this. The app is about control. The Substack is about systems. The dinners are about community. This show is the only thing that contains all three and puts your face and hands on it — cooking, which is what you actually love, for strangers, which is what the country actually needs.

It also fixes the one real weakness in your positioning. Right now Dig In is a founder with a thesis. After a season of this, Dig In is a founder with a hundred hours of documented evidence that she was right. Nobody argues with that.

> "I don't want to build an app that makes people feel less alone from their couch. I want to build the thing that gets them to the table, and then I want to be at the table."

Put that on the About page.
