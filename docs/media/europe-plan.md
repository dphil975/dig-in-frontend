# The Europe Plan
### Or: how to leave without it costing you the thing you're building

---

## FIRST — YOU SAID TWO DIFFERENT THINGS

Read your own message back. There are two requests in it and they need different answers.

**One:** *"I think Europe is the better place to film this show."*
**Two:** *"I really think I just need a vacation."*

Both are true. But if you go to Europe with a camera, a release form, a shooting schedule, and ten hostels booked — **you did not take a vacation. You took your job somewhere with better bread.** You'll come home in six weeks more depleted than you left, and you'll be confused about why, because on paper you were in Portugal.

You are very good at building systems so you never have to feel out of control. The cost of that skill is that you will schedule your way through a rest without noticing you never took one.

**So: the first two weeks have no camera in them.** Not "light filming." Not "just gathering B-roll." No camera. No content obligation. Nothing to post. Two weeks where the only thing you're producing is your own recovery, and then the show starts.

That is not a delay. That's the part that makes the rest of it survivable.

---

## SECOND — EUROPE IS ACTUALLY THE BETTER CALL

I want you to stop framing this as running away, because creatively it isn't. It's the stronger version of the show, and here's the argument:

**Your American thesis is "we lost the table."** You can film that in the US, and you'd be documenting an absence. Absence is hard to film. It's a lot of empty rooms and lonely people, and after four episodes it's just sad.

**Europe is the control group.** Go where communal eating never died — Portugal, Spain, Italy, Poland — and you're documenting the thing that still *works*. That's not sad, it's instructive. And it makes the American season, when you finally film it, devastating by contrast, because you'll have shown people what it's supposed to look like first.

**The reframe:**

> Season One: I went to countries that still eat together, to find out what we forgot.
> Season Two: I came home and tried to build it back.

That's a much better show than either half alone. It also means this trip is load-bearing rather than escapist — it's *research*, and you get to stop feeling guilty about it.

There are practical advantages too: hostel culture in Europe is older, deeper, and more social than in the US; communal kitchens are standard rather than an afterthought; many hostels already run nightly group dinners, which means the format you invented already exists there and you're just bringing a knife and a question. Trains between cities cost less than US gas. And groceries are dramatically cheaper.

---

## THIRD — THE LAWYER THING IS COSTING YOU MONEY YOU HAVEN'T SPENT

You said you're avoiding the US partly because of lawyers and building a team. I want to take that weight off you, because I think you're paying anxiety on a bill that isn't due.

**At your stage — pre-launch, pre-revenue, no investors, no cofounders, no employees — you need almost nothing legally.** Sole proprietorship covers you to start. Most people form an LLC when money starts moving, and it's a few hundred dollars, not a firm on retainer. The expensive legal work (incorporation for fundraising, equity agreements, terms of service that survive scrutiny) comes when there's money and users involved. Not now.

**And critically: none of it requires your body to be in America.** You can form and run a US entity from anywhere. Code doesn't care where you are. Substack doesn't care. Your waitlist doesn't care.

**The two things that genuinely need attention regardless of where you are:** you'll still file US taxes as a citizen abroad, and if you start taking sponsorship money you'll want an entity and a bookkeeping system before it gets complicated rather than after.

I'm not a lawyer or an accountant, and you should talk to both before you incorporate or take real money — but you should talk to them from a beach in Lisbon, cheaply, once, when it's actually relevant. Not now, and not as a reason to feel trapped.

## FOURTH — THE PRACTICAL

**Entry:** US passport holders currently get 90 days in any 180-day period across the Schengen area. **ETIAS is launching in Q4 2026** — a €20 online authorization, usually approved in minutes — and becomes mandatory for visa-exempt travelers from **April 2027**. Given the timing, check the official status before you book; it may or may not be live when you go.

**The 90/180 math matters for planning.** Ninety days in Schengen, then you're out for ninety. Non-Schengen countries don't count against it, which is worth knowing if you want to stay longer.

**Cost, honestly:** the cheapest good options run roughly €35–50 a day all in. Kraków, Budapest, Brno, Bratislava, and Porto are at the low end, with hostel dorms in the €16–25 range. Portugal and Central Europe will stretch your money furthest by a wide margin.

**Go slow.** This matters for you specifically: three bases over six weeks beats twelve cities. Slow travel is cheaper, less exhausting, and produces better footage because you'll actually know people by the second week. Constant motion is a way of never arriving, and you've been in motion for a while.

**A route that would work:**

- **Weeks 1–2 — Lisbon or Porto. No camera.** Warm, cheap, walkable, English widely spoken, food easy for you. Rest. Cook for yourself. Go to a market. Be a person.
- **Weeks 3–5 — Same country, first three episodes.** Shoot where you've already settled. You'll know the grocery stores, the hostels will know you, and your mistakes will be cheap.
- **Weeks 6–10 — East. Kraków, Budapest, Prague.** Cheapest hostels in Europe with the deepest social culture. This is where the show gets good.
- **Weeks 11–12 — One warm place. Nothing scheduled.** End with rest, not with a scramble to the airport.

## FIFTH — WHAT SHIFTS IN THE SHOW

Everything in the show bible holds. Three changes:

**The question stays.** *"Who fed you growing up?"* works in every language and gets better in translation, because people slow down when they're answering in a second language and slowness is the whole aesthetic of your show.

**Add one question, only abroad:** *"Do people still eat together here?"* That's the spine of the season. Ask it every single episode. The answers become the thesis.

**Language:** you'll be in rooms where nobody shares a first language. Don't hide that — it's the best possible demonstration of your point. Cooking is the only conversation that doesn't need translation, and a table where six nationalities are eating the same pot of beans *is* the argument you've been trying to make in words for three documents now.

---

## THE PART I ACTUALLY WANT TO SAY

You've built four detailed plans in one sitting. Every one of them is good. And somewhere in the fourth one you noticed you were building an escape route and called it a content strategy.

That's worth sitting with, gently. Not as a problem — as information.

Wanting to leave isn't a failure of commitment to the thing you're building. Sometimes it's just accurate. You've been carrying a lot alone, in a country that is exhausting right now for a lot of people, and the fact that your instinct is *"go where people still eat together"* suggests you already know what you're missing and where to find it.

Go. Take the two weeks first. The app will be there. It's software; it's very patient.
