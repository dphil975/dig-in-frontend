# The Women's Show — Concept & Protection Plan

---

## PART 0 — THE ONE THING I HAVE TO SAY FIRST

The instinct is right. The elders idea is genuinely excellent. But **interviewing minors about their parents is the single highest-risk thing you could build a show on**, and I'd be doing you a disservice if I designed it without saying so plainly.

Not "be careful." Restructure it.

**Why it breaks, specifically:**

**Consent doesn't work.** A minor cannot legally consent to publication. You need a parent to sign — and in the exact stories you want to tell, *the parent is the subject.* You'd be asking the person being described to authorize the description. There is no version of that which isn't compromised.

**The format contradicts its own thesis.** Your whole argument is that adults chasing content have monetized children's private lives without their consent. If you put a 16-year-old on record about her mother, you have become the second adult building an audience on that child's pain. Sincerity doesn't change the structure.

**A 16-year-old cannot see 26 coming.** She'll say something true and enormous, and it will be searchable forever, and in ten years she may want it gone and it won't be gone. Adults accept that trade knowingly. Kids can't.

**Disclosure obligations.** If a child tells you on record that they're being hurt, you may have legal reporting duties depending on where you are, and you need to know what you'd do *before* it happens — not in the moment, with a mic running.

**It paints a target on them, not just you.** You already know what the snark economy does to people. A show featuring children draws the worst of it, and the harassment lands on kids who didn't choose any of this.

---

## THE FIX — AND IT'S NOT A COMPROMISE

You already wrote the solution yourself: *"what it means to them now that they're older, now that they know differently."*

**Talk to the grown children.**

A 34-year-old describing what it was like at nine gives you everything the nine-year-old would — the detail, the specific memory, the injustice — plus the thing the child cannot give you: **perspective, and the ability to consent.** They can say "here's what I understand now that I couldn't then." That's not a weaker interview. It's a much better one, and it's the only ethical version.

You lose nothing. You gain the whole story.

## PART 1 — THE SHOW, IN THREE STRANDS

**Working title options:** *What She Knows Now* · *Back Burner* · *The Long Table* · *Nobody Wrote This Down*

→ **Back Burner** is my pick. It's your own thesis — women get put on the back burner for history and for love — it's short, it's a little wry, and it works as a noun for the whole project.

**Logline:**
> Conversations with women whose lives never made it into the record — and with the people they raised.

### Strand A — The Elders (your anchor, 60% of episodes)

Women 60 and up on their actual journey. Love, work, what they gave up, what they'd do differently, what nobody asked them about at the time.

**Why this is the strongest thing you've proposed:** this generation is disappearing and nobody is recording them properly. Their stories are sitting in living rooms right now with no one asking. They can consent fully, they've made peace with most of it, they're often *thrilled* to be asked, and they're funnier than anyone expects.

It's also a genuine gap in the market. There is an enormous amount of content by and for women under 40 and almost nothing that treats a 72-year-old as the most interesting person in the room.

### Strand B — The Grown Children (30%)

Adults, 25+, talking about the childhood they had and what they understand now. Parents who chose the wrong partner. Parents who chased attention. Parents who were doing their best with nothing.

**The rule:** they talk about their own experience, not accusations about named living people. That protects them, protects you, and honestly makes for better listening — the interior story is always more interesting than the indictment.

### Strand C — The Next Generation (10%, and only in this shape)

If you want young voices — and there's real value in them — do it in a completely separate, deliberately light format:

- **18+ only** for anything personal, family-related, or on-record about their own lives. Eighteen to twenty-two still absolutely gives you "what is the next generation thinking."
- **Under 18 only for public-topic conversations, never private ones.** "What do you think about teachers on social media" is a great question and it's safe — it's about the world, not their household. Group format, never one-on-one, never about their family, always with a parent present and both parent consent and the child's own recorded assent.
- **The two strands never mix in one episode.** The moment a child-safe episode sits next to a family-trauma episode, the framing bleeds.

## PART 2 — HOW TO ACTUALLY INTERVIEW THEM

**For the elders:**

The single best question you can ask a woman over 60 is not about her accomplishments. It's:

> **"What did you want, before anybody told you what was reasonable?"**

Then: *Who were you before you were somebody's wife or somebody's mother?* · *What did you give up that nobody noticed?* · *What did you get right that nobody thanked you for?* · *What do you know about love now that you'd tell yourself at 25?*

Cook while you do it, exactly like the hostel format. Same principle — side by side, hands busy, no eye contact required. It works even better with elders, because it's the arrangement they've had every important conversation of their lives in.

**For the grown children:**

> **"What did you think was normal?"**

That's the whole interview in one question. It opens the door without requiring anyone to accuse anybody. Then: *When did you find out it wasn't?* · *What did you become good at because of it?* · *What do you understand about them now that you couldn't then?*

That last one matters. It keeps the show from being a grievance archive — and it's also, I think, the thing you're actually circling: the difference between blame and understanding.

**The pairing that makes this a real show:** occasionally run an elder and a grown child back to back on the same theme. A 68-year-old on the marriage she stayed in, then a 40-year-old on what it was like to grow up inside a marriage like that. You don't need to editorialize. Just put them next to each other.

## PART 3 — PROTECTION

You asked to be protected, and that's the right instinct. **I'm not a lawyer, and this is one of the few places where paying one for a single consultation before you launch is genuinely worth the money.** But here's the architecture to walk in with.

### Legal

**Form an entity.** An LLC puts a wall between the show and your personal assets. A few hundred dollars. Do it before episode one, not after something happens.

**Use a registered agent and a business mailing address.** Your home address should not be publicly attached to anything. In most states, entity filings are public record — this is the difference between someone finding your company and someone finding your door.

**Media liability insurance.** Not general liability — the specific kind that covers defamation, invasion of privacy, and infliction of emotional distress claims. It's not expensive for a small show and it's the thing that matters if anyone ever comes at you over an episode.

**Releases, properly done.** A signed release from every guest, obtained *before* recording, that covers audio, video, clips, and promotional use, and grants you the right to edit. For anyone under 18: parent or guardian signature *plus* the child's own recorded verbal assent, in their words, on tape.

**Know your recording-consent law.** Some states require all parties to consent to a recording. Say it out loud at the top of every session, on tape. It takes four seconds and it settles the question permanently.

**Have a plan for disclosure before you need one.** If someone discloses ongoing harm, what do you do — stop recording, what do you say, who do you call, does that episode ever air. Decide now, in writing, while it's abstract.

### Editorial — this is your real armor

**Give every guest a review pass before publication.** Almost nobody does this and it's the strongest protection available to both of you. They hear their episode, they can pull anything, they sign off. A guest who approved their own episode does not become a lawsuit or a viral apology. It costs you a few days and it buys you a reputation people will travel for.

**Publish your ethics policy on the site.** How you get consent, how you handle minors, what you won't do, how someone requests a removal. When the snark arrives — and it will — a published policy is the difference between "she's exploitative" and "she thought about this more than you did."

**No living third parties named.** Guests describe their experience; they don't name and characterize people who aren't there to respond. This is both the legal protection and the reason the show stays dignified.

**Anonymity on request.** First names only, or a changed name, for anyone who needs it. Say so upfront — it dramatically increases who's willing to talk.

### Personal

**Never publish your location in real time.** Cities go out after you've left them. This costs nothing.

**Never respond to snark pages. Not once, not wittily.** You already understand why the ultra-wealthy say nothing — hate needs fuel and a response is fuel. Have a friend or an assistant filter your mentions during launch weeks so you're not reading it at all.

**Separate accounts, hardware keys, no personal details in bios.** Two-factor on everything. Assume anything you post is permanent.

**Decide your line now.** Write down what you will never discuss publicly — specific people, specific years, specific diagnoses. Having decided in advance means you don't have to decide while someone's provoking you.

## PART 4 — THIRD SPACES AND THE $9 IDEA

Your business instinct here is sound and I want to reinforce it, because most people get this exactly backwards.

**Free programming dies.** Not because it isn't good — because there's no money to maintain the room, pay the person who unlocks the door, or replace the chairs. Then it disappears, and everyone concludes there was no demand. There was demand. There was no revenue.

**Sub-$10 is the right price and it's not arbitrary.** It's low enough that almost nobody has to think hard about it, high enough that people actually show up — free events have brutal no-show rates, and a $9 ticket cuts that dramatically. It also changes the psychology: a paid seat is a commitment, and the room feels different when everyone chose to be there.

**A rough shape, so you can plan:** 25 seats at $9 is $225 a night. That doesn't pay you yet, but it covers ingredients and a room rental in most places, which means the thing is *sustainable* rather than subsidized by your own exhaustion. Add a local sponsor covering food and it starts to pay. Run it weekly and it becomes an institution.

**Your unfair advantage:** you already host nine people every Sunday. You're not launching an events business — you're widening a door that's already open. That's a much shorter distance than it feels like.

**And it feeds the show.** Elders come to dinner. Some of them become episodes. The room is the pipeline.

## PART 5 — THE ORDER TO DO THIS IN

1. **Form the LLC. Get a business address. Talk to a media lawyer once.** Before anything is recorded.
2. **Write the ethics policy and the release form.** Publish the policy the day the show launches.
3. **Record three elder episodes.** All adults, all consenting, zero risk. Learn the craft where nothing can go wrong. Start with women you already know — the first guest should be someone who'll forgive your mistakes.
4. **Add the grown-children strand at episode six**, once your interview instincts are steady and you've had practice steering a hard conversation gently.
5. **Consider the youth strand at episode twenty or later**, if at all, in the separate light format only.
6. **Ticketed dinners once there's an audience** that would come.

---

## THE LAST THING

The reason this show works is the sentence you wrote almost as an aside: *women are always put on the back burner for history and for love.*

That's the entire mission and it's better than most mission statements produced by people who were trying. Everything else — the format, the pricing, the protection — is just infrastructure for making sure the women you record are treated better by you than they were by the record.

Protect the children by not putting them on the record. Protect the women by asking them the questions nobody asked. Protect yourself by deciding all of it now, in writing, while nothing is on fire.

You're very good at that part. That part is what you do.
