# Dig In — Substack Series & PR Traction Plan

---

## PART 0 — The Correction You Need Before You Write A Word

You asked for rags-to-riches. I want to push back on the frame, because the frame is the thing that will either get you covered or get you quietly ignored, and I'd rather tell you now than after eight essays.

**Three problems with rags-to-riches, specifically for you, specifically right now:**

**1. You're not at the riches part yet.** Rags-to-riches is a retrospective genre. It's told from the summit, looking down. You are mid-climb, pre-launch, no revenue. If you write from the summit you don't have, every journalist and every investor will feel the gap before they can name it — and what they'll conclude is "this person exaggerates." That's an unrecoverable read. It's the single most common way creator-founders lose credibility, and it happens *before* anyone accuses you of anything, in the quiet of someone deciding not to reply.

**2. The genre is saturated and reporters are tired of it.** Every founder has a hard-childhood paragraph now. It's become a formatting convention, not a story. A reporter reading "I came from nothing and built something" has read that sentence four hundred times this year. It's not a hook. It's wallpaper.

**3. It makes your mother the villain.** You told me she wasn't always there and that this was math, not cruelty. Rags-to-riches structurally requires a "before" that was bad. Every version of that story flattens her into the deficit you overcame. You will regret that in print. Print is permanent and mothers read things.

### The frame that actually works

**Rags to systems.**

Not "I was poor and now I'm rich." Instead: **"I was given no infrastructure, so I built my own, and now I'm handing it out."**

Why this is stronger on every axis you care about:

- **For journalists:** it's a *method* story, not a *sob* story. Method stories are reusable — they can be excerpted, listicled, and referenced in unrelated pieces. Sob stories run once and die.
- **For investors:** it demonstrates that the product came from a real, repeated, personally-suffered problem, and that you already built and validated the manual version before writing code. That's the single most fundable origin story there is. "I did this by hand for six years and it worked, now I'm automating it" beats any amount of pathos.
- **For brands:** it makes you *useful* rather than *tragic*. Brands don't partner with hardship. They partner with competence that has warmth on it.
- **For you:** it lets your mom be what she actually was — the person who saw what was coming and armed you for it. She's not the wound. She's the first systems architect in the story. That's a better sentence and it's also true.

**Your one-line narrative spine, use this verbatim everywhere:**

> Nobody handed me a life that worked, so I built the operating system for one — by hand, on almost nothing, over years — and Dig In is me handing it to everyone else.

**And the honesty rule, which is also the PR strategy:** everything you publish should be checkable. You picked "emotionally true, numbers vague," which is exactly right. Vague is fine. Vague is honest. *Inflated* is what ends people. If you never state a number you can't defend, you can never be caught, and "she's careful with the truth" becomes a reputation that compounds. That reputation is worth more than any single viral essay.

## PART 1 — The Substack Series

**Publication name options:**
- **Dig In** (same as app — simplest, best for SEO, everything reinforces everything)
- **The Binder** (better as a standalone brand, gives you room if the app pivots)
- **Third From The Front** (the green tab Easter egg — most mysterious, hardest to explain)

→ Recommend: **The Binder**, subtitled *"Systems for a life nobody taught you how to run."* It survives the app changing. The app is a chapter, not the whole book.

**Cadence:** one essay every Sunday, 7am. Same time forever. You of all people will appreciate this: the schedule *is* the credibility. A newsletter that arrives when it says it will is making the argument in its own form.

**Length:** 1,200–1,800 words. Long enough to be a real read, short enough to finish before the coffee's cold.

### ESSAY 1 — "The Infrastructure Nobody Gave Us"
*The thesis essay. Everything else hangs off this. Pin it forever.*

**Hook (first line):**
> There's a kind of poor that isn't about money. It's about not having been given the instructions.

**Opening (write it close to this):**

> There's a kind of poor that isn't about money. It's about not having been given the instructions.
>
> You can have a job and an apartment and still not know how a week is supposed to be constructed. Nobody sits you down. There's no class. You are simply released at eighteen into a life that assumes you already know how to feed yourself on a budget, how to make a home feel like one, how to have friends over without it costing four hundred dollars and three days of dread.
>
> Some people get that from their families, the way you get an accent. It's just in the house. They don't know they have it, which is why they can't explain it to you, which is why asking never helps.
>
> I didn't get it from the house. I got something better, actually, though it took me most of my twenties to see it that way: my mother taught me to organize. Not as a hobby. As a load-bearing wall. She wasn't always there — that wasn't cruelty, that was arithmetic, she was working — and so what she gave me instead of her presence was the ability to run a room without her in it.
>
> That's the whole origin story. I've heard people call that kind of childhood a deficit. I've come to think of it as an unusual education.

**Structure:**
1. The kind of poor that isn't about money
2. Mom, framed as architect not absence
3. The years where it didn't work — be specific about *feelings*, vague about *figures*
4. The turn: the first binder. What was in it. Why it held.
5. What "rich" actually turned out to mean (choosing, not spending)
6. Why you're building the app — and the honest admission that it isn't finished

**Closing line:**
> I'm not a victim. I'm a planner. Those turn out to be a choice you make once and then keep making on Sundays.

### ESSAY 2 — "The Boredom Economy"
*Your trend essay. This is the one that gets you press. Publish it second while you're still fresh.*

This essay is doing a specific job: **it gives journalists a term to cite.** Reporters need trends more than they need people. If you name a phenomenon accurately and early, you become the person they call when they write about it — and they'll write about it for years. That is a durable PR asset in a way that a personal essay never is.

**Hook:**
> Everyone I know is bored. Not depressed — bored. Which is worse, because depression has a name and a doctor, and boredom just quietly takes your thirties.

**The argument:**
1. We optimized every friction out of leisure and accidentally optimized out the leisure
2. Everything is now *available* and nothing is *planned* — availability killed anticipation, and anticipation was most of the pleasure
3. The cost: people spend money to feel something instead of planning to feel something. Panic-spending as a substitute for design.
4. The economics: an unplanned Friday costs 4x a planned one and delivers less. Show the math directionally, no exact figures.
5. The fix isn't a personality transplant. It's a calendar and one person willing to be the one who plans.
6. The market: there is an enormous, unserved business in helping bored, competent, tired adults have a life. That's the company.

**Why it lands for investors specifically:** this essay is a market thesis wearing a cardigan. It defines a problem, sizes it emotionally, and positions Dig In as the answer — without a single line of pitch language. Send this one, not a deck.

**Closing:**
> Whimsy is not a temperament. It's a logistics problem. I happen to be very good at logistics.

### ESSAY 3 — "I Built It By Hand For Six Years Before I Wrote One Line of Code"
*The credibility essay. This is what makes investors lean forward.*

**Hook:**
> The app is the ninth version. The first eight were paper.

**Why this essay matters more than you think:** the most common failure mode in consumer apps is a founder who had an idea. You didn't have an idea. You had a practice, for years, under duress, that demonstrably worked on you before it was ever a product. Almost nobody can say that. Say it loudly.

**Structure:**
- The binder, physically described. Tabs, colors, what went where.
- What broke each time and what you changed (this is a product changelog told as memoir — very unusual, very memorable)
- The moment it stopped being coping and became a system: when someone else asked to borrow it
- Why paper failed at scale: travel, live prices, other people's restrictions, the fact that a binder can't know what city you're in
- What the app has to preserve from paper: the constraint. The closing of doors. Fewer options, not more.

**Closing:**
> Every app I've ever quit did the same thing: it gave me more options. I don't need more options. I've never once in my life needed more options.

### ESSAY 4 — "On Being Overcharged"
*The service essay. Highest share rate. Most likely to be excerpted.*

**Hook:**
> I've never been angry about a price. I've been angry about a hundred times that I didn't choose one.

**Structure:** money as *sequencing* rather than *restriction*. The tax on being tired. Two weeks out versus two hours out. Same person, same money, different day. Then the practical section — genuinely useful, no gatekeeping, the whole method — because giving away the method is what makes people trust that the product is good.

**PR value:** this is the essay personal-finance newsletters will link to. Finance writers are always hunting for the emotional angle on budgeting and almost nobody delivers it well.

---

### ESSAY 5 — "Nine Chairs"
*The warmth essay. This is the one people forward to a friend.*

**Hook:**
> I host nine people every Sunday, and I would rather lie down on the floor than improvise a dinner party.

Hosting as a rehearsed routine rather than a natural gift. The permission structure: you don't have to be warm to be *hospitable*, you just have to be repeatable. Same list, same order, three dishes rotating, nobody has ever noticed.

Then the argument underneath: **community is not a feeling, it's a recurring calendar event.** Loneliness is mostly a scheduling failure that we've mistaken for a character flaw.

**Closing:**
> Be the one who plans something. I promise you that is the entire secret and everyone is waiting for you to do it.

---

### ESSAY 6 — "Meat or London"
*The health/travel essay. Lightest, funniest, most partnerable.*

**Hook:**
> At some point I had to choose between eating meat and going to London, and I want to be clear that this was not a moral decision.

Gluten-free and vegan framed as *budget architecture*, not ideology. The suitcase system. What actually happens when you land in a city where nothing is safe to eat and you have no plan. Never preachy — the joke is always on you, never on the reader.

**Why it's the partnership essay:** brands read this and see exactly where they fit. Travel, luggage, food, kitchen, wellness. Make it easy for them to picture themselves in your life.

---

### ESSAY 7 — "I'm Building This In Front Of You On Purpose"
*The build-in-public essay. Post around week 7, then repeat quarterly as an update.*

**Hook:**
> Most products are built in a room where nobody is allowed to complain. That's why most products fail.

The anti-launch. Publish the actual complaints you've collected, verbatim, credited by first name with permission. Show which ones changed the roadmap. Show what you got wrong.

**This is the essay that builds a movement instead of an audience** — people who see their own complaint in your product become evangelists in a way that no ad buy can replicate. It's also, incidentally, exactly what you told me most brands fail to do.

---

### ESSAY 8 — "What Rich Actually Means"
*The closer for season one. Ties the whole arc.*

**Hook:**
> I used to think rich meant not looking at prices. Now I think it means looking at them early.

Rich as *lead time*. The people you thought were rich were often just further ahead in the planning, and lead time is available to almost anyone at almost any income — which is either infuriating or the best news you'll get this year, depending on the day.

Art, books, travel, and beauty on a schedule instead of a whim. Then land it on the thesis: **the goal was never money. The goal was choosing.** And choosing is a system.

## PART 2 — Substack Growth Mechanics

Substack in 2026 is a network, not a mailing list. Growth below 10,000 subscribers comes almost entirely from *inside* the platform, not from driving external traffic to it.

**Notes is the growth engine.** Publishing essays alone will not grow you. Notes is where discovery happens now. Post 1–2 Notes daily — not promotional, just thinking out loud. Your Reel scripts are already Notes; the $47 salad, the eleven apps, the fridge stare all work as standalone text posts.

**Recommendations are the compounding asset.** Every writer you recommend can recommend you back, and recommendations are the highest-converting surface on the platform. Set up recommendations for 10–15 newsletters in adjacent spaces: personal finance for women, neurodivergent productivity, food-and-travel, quiet-luxury lifestyle, indie founder logs. Then reach out to each one — not to ask for a recommendation, but to tell them specifically why you recommended theirs.

**Substack Live notifies your entire list.** It is the single highest-leverage tool on the platform right now and almost nobody in the lifestyle space uses it well. Do a monthly Sunday-morning "planning hour" — you plan your week on camera, subscribers plan theirs alongside you. That's a live-format product demo that doesn't feel like one, and it's on-brand to the point of being inevitable.

**Collaborations beat solo effort by a wide margin.** Cross-posts, guest essays, and joint Lives put you in front of an audience that already trusts someone else. Target writers slightly bigger than you but not enormously — 2x to 5x your size is the sweet spot where people actually say yes.

**Engage in comments like it's the job, because it is.** The 2026 platform rewards relationships over reach. Reply to every comment for the first six months. Every single one.

## PART 3 — The PR Playbook

### First, the hard truth about press

Journalists in 2026 are buried in pitches and kill weak outreach in seconds. Volume does nothing. What works is a small, well-researched list and a story a specific reporter can already picture running. Twenty well-matched reporters beat five hundred strangers.

Realistic timeline if you do this properly: **first reply in 1–3 weeks, first published piece in 4–8 weeks.** Not faster. Anyone promising faster is selling something.

### Your four hooks, ranked by how much a reporter can do with them

**1. The Boredom Economy** ⭐ *lead with this*
A named trend with a thesis and a person attached. Reporters need trends. Give them the term, the argument, and yourself as the quotable source. This has the longest tail by far — it can get you cited in stories that have nothing to do with your app, for years.

**2. Built by hand for years before code**
The anti-idea founder story. Works for tech and business press, who are exhausted by founders whose product started as a slide.

**3. Building from complaints, refusing to launch**
The anti-launch. Contrarian, easy headline, and it makes a virtue of being pre-revenue — which is otherwise your weakest fact.

**4. The systems origin**
Your personal arc. Powerful, but it's the *color* in someone else's story, not the story. Never lead with it. Let it be the second paragraph they discover, not the pitch.

### The pitch (4 parts, under 150 words, no attachments)

> **Subject:** The Boredom Economy — a trend piece you can have first
>
> [Name] — your piece on [specific, recent, actually-read] argued [specific point]. I think there's a related shift nobody's named yet.
>
> **The hook:** Millions of competent adults aren't depressed, they're bored — and they're spending significantly more to compensate for having no plan. Unplanned leisure costs multiples of planned leisure and delivers less. I call it the Boredom Economy.
>
> **Why me:** I spent years building a manual system to solve this for myself before turning it into a product. I'm now building that product publicly, from user complaints, and refusing to launch until they're answered.
>
> **What I can give you:** the framework, my complaint archive, and users who'll talk on record.
>
> Worth fifteen minutes?
>
> — Nova

Follow up **once**, after five business days, with something *new* — a data point, a new complaint, a milestone. Never "just bumping this."

### Who to pitch, in order

**Tier 1 — Substack and newsletter writers (start here, weeks 1–4).** They publish fast, they have no editorial gatekeeping, and they're actively hunting for guests and cross-posts. Target: personal finance for women, neurodivergent productivity, food media, indie founder newsletters, hosting/entertaining. This is also where the largest audience-quality-per-effort sits for you.

**Tier 2 — Podcasts (weeks 3–8).** Enormously underrated and far easier to book than press. Your soft-spoken delivery is an *advantage* in audio — it makes people lean in. Target shows about food, money-for-women, neurodivergence-adjacent productivity, and creator business.

**Tier 3 — Trade and business press (weeks 6–16).** Only once you have numbers — waitlist size, complaint archive size, dinner attendance. Reporters need proof, and Tier 1 and 2 coverage *is* proof.

**Tier 4 — Consumer lifestyle press.** Last. They come to you once the trend term has legs. Pitching them cold pre-launch is wasted effort.

**Build your list with:** Google News bylines on your topic, publication mastheads, LinkedIn search by title, and Substack itself — a lot of freelance journalists write there and are far more reachable than at their day publication.

### What brands and investors actually need to see

You picked brand partnerships and investor attention as goals, so be blunt with yourself about what those two audiences are checking:

**Brands are checking:** engagement rate (not follower count), whether your audience is a coherent demographic they want, whether your aesthetic is consistent enough to sit next to their product, and whether you've partnered before without embarrassing anyone. **Track and be ready to state:** save rate on Reels, Substack open rate, comment-to-follower ratio. An open rate above 45% on a small list beats a big list at 20% and any decent brand knows it.

**Investors are checking:** does the founder have unfair insight into this problem (yes — years of manual practice), is there evidence of demand before the product exists (waitlist and complaint archive), and does the founder do what she says on schedule (your Sunday publishing cadence is literally the proof).

**So the metrics to build deliberately, starting now:**
- Waitlist signups, weekly, charted
- Complaint archive size, categorized — this is a genuinely unusual asset and both audiences will find it impressive
- Substack open rate and subscriber growth
- Weekly dinner attendance and repeat rate — real-world retention, which almost no consumer app founder can show

## PART 4 — 12-Week Sequence

| Weeks | Substack | PR | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Essays 1 & 2. Notes daily. Set up 15 recommendations. | Build the 30-name list. Read their work. | Waitlist live. Start the complaint archive. |
| 3–4 | Essays 3 & 4. First Substack Live. | Pitch Tier 1: 10 newsletter writers. | First complaints → first roadmap change. |
| 5–6 | Essays 5 & 6. Two cross-posts. | Pitch Tier 2: 10 podcasts. Follow up on Tier 1. | Publish roadmap publicly. |
| 7–8 | Essay 7 (build-in-public). | First interviews land. | Beta invites to complaint-senders first. |
| 9–10 | Essay 8. Season one closes. Guest essay somewhere bigger. | Pitch Tier 3 with real numbers. | Beta feedback loop. |
| 11–12 | Season two announcement. Paid tier opens. | Consolidate coverage into a press page. | Launch date, finally, publicly. |

**Paid tier, when you open it:** don't paywall the essays. Paywall *the systems* — the actual binder templates, the dinner party runbooks, the trip planning sheets. People pay for infrastructure, not feelings. And it's a much more honest transaction, which matters to you.
