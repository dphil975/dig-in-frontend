# Dig In — The Money Playbook
### Brands, affiliates, sponsors, and what actually pays pre-launch

---

## PART 0 — Four Things You're Treating As One Thing

You said "brands I can reach out to as investors or advertisers." Those are two completely different transactions, and there are actually four revenue streams tangled together here. Separating them is the difference between sounding like someone who's done this and someone who hasn't — and you're going to be in rooms where that read happens in the first thirty seconds.

| Stream | What it is | What it needs | Realistic for you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Affiliate** | You link, they buy, you get a cut | Nothing. Zero followers. | ✅ **Start Monday** |
| **Brand sponsorship** | Brand pays you to post | Audience + engagement proof | 🟡 3–6 months |
| **App ad revenue** | Ads inside Dig In | Thousands of daily users | 🔴 Much later than you think |
| **Investment** | Someone buys equity | Traction, not a nice email | 🔴 Different sport entirely |

### The correction that matters most

**A brand paying you is not an investor. A brand paying you is a customer buying advertising.** They get a post, you get a check, nobody owns anything. Investors buy a piece of your company and expect it back multiplied. If you email a brand asking them to "invest or advertise," a marketing manager reads that and immediately knows you haven't done this before — and marketing managers talk.

Corporate venture arms *do* invest in startups (Unilever, Nestlé, and General Mills all have them). But they invest in companies with users and revenue, sourced through warm introductions, not cold outreach. Park that. It's a year-two conversation and it'll be a much easier one once you have numbers.

### The honest math on app ads

You mentioned Google ads. Here's what nobody tells you before you build around it: US mobile ad rates run roughly **$8–$15 per thousand impressions**, and Google keeps about **32%** of that. Outside Tier 1 countries it collapses to well under a dollar.

Run the numbers on a good day: 1,000 daily active users, three ad impressions each = 3,000 impressions. At a strong $12 eCPM that's $36, minus Google's cut, so about **$24 a day.** With a thousand people using your app daily — which would be a real achievement.

Pinterest, for comparison, has hundreds of millions of monthly users. Ad-supported consumer apps are a *volume* business, and volume is the one thing you don't have and won't have for a long time.

**So here's the strategic point:** for your specific audience, **commerce economics beat ad economics by an order of magnitude.** A woman planning a dinner party through your app might buy $80 of groceries and a $40 serving bowl. At 8% affiliate commission that's ~$9.60 from one person, one time — versus about a *quarter of a cent* from showing her an ad. Your users are high-intent shoppers in a high-ticket category. Don't monetize them like they're playing a puzzle game.

**Dig In should be an affiliate business with a subscription on top, not an ad business.** That's also a much better story for investors later, because it means you're not dependent on scale you haven't earned.

### TIER 0 — Join this week. No followers required. No permission needed.

These are networks — master keys that unlock hundreds of brands each. Apply to all of them in one sitting. Block three hours, make it a Sunday, put on the binder energy.

| Network | Barrier | Why it matters for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Amazon Associates** | None to join. Must make 3 qualifying sales in 180 days or the account closes. | Everything is on Amazon. Low rates (1–10%) but it converts because people already have accounts. Your kitchen tools, books, and organizational supplies live here. |
| **ShareASale / Awin** | Free, per-brand approval | Now merged into one platform. 4,500+ programs, thousands of travel merchants. This is your workhorse. |

| **Mavely** | Open enrollment, no minimum | Up to ~30% on partner brands. Genuinely no gate. |
| **Skimlinks** | Accepts any size | Auto-converts links in your Substack. Set it and forget it — good for essays. |

**LTK requires ~5,000 followers**, an application, and posting at least twice a week. Put it on the calendar for month four, not now.

**Do this first, before any of it:** get a ShopMy referral link from a creator you already follow. Acceptance is meaningfully easier that way, and asking is a warm first contact with someone in your space.

### TIER 2 — The one nobody thinks of, and the fastest actual cash

**Your weekly dinners and cooking classes are a sponsorable physical asset that requires zero follower count.**

This is the real low-barrier bag and I want you to sit with it for a second. A national brand won't pay a creator with 800 followers. But a **local** brand — a gluten-free bakery, a natural wine shop, a ceramicist, a small-batch olive oil company, a regional meal-kit startup — will absolutely put product on a table of nine engaged women every single Sunday, in exchange for tagged content and a mention.

Why it works when nothing else does yet:
- You're selling **real-world access**, not impressions, and real-world access can't be faked or bought cheaply
- Local brands have small budgets but *fast* decisions — often one person, often the founder, often a yes within a week
- It's recurring by nature, which means it's a relationship rather than a transaction
- It generates content that makes you legible to the *national* brands later

**Start with:** every independent food business within 20 miles that serves your dietary niche. Walk in. Talk to the owner. Being physically present converts at a rate cold email cannot touch, and your soft-spoken, prepared, binder-carrying presentation is going to land extremely well in a small business.

**Then:** your ticketed community events. Sponsorship for an event with a real date, a real venue, and a real headcount is a far easier ask than sponsorship of a feed.

## PART 2 — Outreach Templates

### A. Affiliate application (when there's a free-text field)

Most people leave this blank. Filling it in well is a real edge.

> I write **The Binder**, a weekly newsletter about systems for food, hosting, and travel on a real budget, and I'm building **Dig In**, an app in the same space. My audience is gluten-free and vegan women who plan ahead and buy deliberately.
>
> I don't do hauls. I write one honest recommendation at a time, usually inside a piece about how I actually use the thing. My readers convert because they trust that I've used it for months before mentioning it.
>
> [Newsletter link] · [Instagram link]

### B. The gifted-to-paid ladder (this is the actual path, and it takes months)

Nobody goes from 800 followers to a paid contract in one email. The ladder is:

**Rung 1 — Buy it and post it unprompted.** Tag them. No ask, no DM, nothing. Do this two or three times over a month. You are now a customer they've noticed rather than a stranger asking for money.

**Rung 2 — The gifted ask.** After you've posted organically:

> Subject: I've been posting about [product] on my own — want to make it official?
>
> Hi [Name] —
>
> I've featured [product] three times in the last month without being asked, because it's genuinely in my rotation. [Link, link, link.]
>
> I write a weekly newsletter about systems for eating well on a budget, and I host a dinner for nine women every Sunday. Your product would sit naturally in both.
>
> Would you be open to sending product for a dedicated feature? I'll share performance either way — I keep records on everything, obsessively.
>
> — Nova

**Rung 3 — The paid ask.** Only after a gifted collaboration went well *and you sent them a results report they didn't ask for.* That unrequested report is what separates you from every other creator they work with, and it's the thing that converts gifted into paid.

### C. Local event sponsorship (highest conversion, do this first)

> Subject: Nine women, every Sunday, in [city]
>
> Hi [Name] —
>
> I host a dinner for nine every Sunday — gluten-free, plant-based, mostly women in their 20s and 30s who cook and care where things come from. Same group, every week.
>
> I'd love to feature [product] at an upcoming one. Everything gets photographed and shared, and I'll send you the content to use however you want.
>
> No fee. I'm looking for the right products, not a payday. If it goes well I'd want to talk about something ongoing.
>
> Happy to come by — I'm local.
>
> — Nova

**Why this converts:** it's small, specific, low-risk, and you're not asking for money. It's the single easiest yes in this entire document.

### D. The results report (your secret weapon)

After *every* collaboration, paid or not, send a one-pager: reach, saves, comments, click-throughs, sample screenshots of real comments, and one honest line about what didn't work.

Almost no creator does this. It costs you thirty minutes and it is the reason brands come back with budget. It's also — not incidentally — completely native to how you already operate.

## PART 3 — What To Build Instead Of Ads

If you want to rival Pinterest's scaling, look at how Pinterest actually makes money: it's a **shopping surface**, not a billboard. People arrive with intent to buy and it takes a cut. That model is available to you at a *far* smaller scale than display advertising, because it pays per transaction rather than per eyeball.

**The Dig In revenue stack, in build order:**

1. **Affiliate on every recommendation.** Meal plan generates a grocery list → list links out → you earn. This should exist in version one. It's the whole business.
2. **Subscription for the systems.** Free tier plans your food. Paid tier gives you the dinner party runbooks, trip sheets, and hosting templates. People pay for infrastructure.
3. **Brand-sponsored templates.** "Sunday dinner for nine, presented by [brand]." Native, useful, sells for real money once you have users — and doesn't feel like an ad because it isn't one.
4. **Ads.** Last. Optional. Possibly never.

## PART 4 — The Investor Track, Kept Separate

Don't mix this into brand outreach. Different people, different language, different year.

**What you need before the first real conversation:** waitlist numbers with a growth curve, the complaint archive (genuinely unusual — most founders have opinions, you'd have evidence), weekly dinner attendance and repeat rate as real-world retention, and your publishing consistency as proof you execute.

**Where it starts when you're ready:** angel investors who know your customer, not VCs. Women who've built consumer or food brands. Warm introductions only, always. Your Substack is the introduction — the Boredom Economy essay does more investor work than a deck would, because it demonstrates thinking rather than claiming it.

**Timeline:** approach after you have a working product and 1,000+ engaged waitlist signups. Earlier than that, you're trading a lot of equity for a little money, and you'll be angry about it for years.

## PART 5 — The 90-Day Money Sequence

**Weeks 1–2 — Infrastructure.** Apply to all Tier 0 networks in one sitting. Get a ShopMy referral link. Set up Skimlinks on Substack. Build a link-in-bio storefront. Make the tracking spreadsheet: program, date applied, status, rate, cookie window, first payout.

**Weeks 3–4 — Local.** Walk into ten independent food businesses. Pitch the Sunday dinner. Expect two yes, six maybe, two no. Two is a win.

**Weeks 5–8 — Organic seeding.** Post about products you already own and love, unprompted, tagging brands. Build the record you'll cite later. Meanwhile the affiliate links start earning quietly in the background.

**Weeks 9–12 — First asks.** Gifted outreach to the brands you've been posting about. Send results reports for anything that lands. First ticketed event with a local sponsor attached.

**Realistic expectation:** first ninety days is likely a few hundred dollars, not a few thousand. Amazon's three-sales-in-180-days requirement will be the first real milestone and it'll feel small. The point of this quarter isn't the money — it's building the *proof* that makes month six through twelve worth multiples of it.

**The one thing to track above all others:** click-through rate on your affiliate links. Not follower count. A small audience that clicks is worth more to a brand than a large one that scrolls, and CTR is the single number that gets you paid.
