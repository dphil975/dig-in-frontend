# The Ecosystem & Money Plan
### Podcast · Magazine · App — and where the money actually comes from

## FIRST, ONE HONEST THING

You asked me to make sure you get the money. I can't promise you that, and anyone who does is selling you something. What I can do is build you a plan with real numbers, tell you which parts are likely and which are hopeful, and be straight about the timelines.

The good news is that your instinct here is better than you know, and the reason isn't the one you think.

## PART 0 — THE REFRAME

You think you're building three things. You're building **one asset, expressed three ways.**

The asset is: **hundreds of women, on record, telling you verbatim what they actually want.**

That's not content. That's primary research — and it is the most valuable thing in this entire plan. You said it yourself and then moved right past it:

> *"advertisers don't really know what the younger generation wants."*

They don't. And they spend enormous amounts of money trying to find out. Research firms are collecting a million survey responses a day, selling behavioral reports on how young people search, shop and decide. There is a whole industry built on guessing at this.

But all of that is **quantitative** — panels, click data, survey checkboxes. What none of it captures is a 24-year-old and a 61-year-old sitting in a kitchen answering *what do you actually want out of your life,* unhurried, in their own words, to someone who cares about the answer.

**Nobody has that. You'd have it on tape.**

So the hierarchy is:

> **Interviews** (the asset)
> → **Podcast** (distribution, free, builds the audience)
> → **Magazine** (the premium object, the archive, the thing people pay for)
> → **App** (the systems, the recurring revenue)
> → **Insight** (what brands eventually pay the most for)

Everything is one thing. That's why it works, and it's why you won't burn out maintaining three businesses — you're doing one interview and cutting it four ways.

## PART 2 — THE MAGAZINE, DONE SO IT DOESN'T BANKRUPT YOU

The magazine is a genuinely great idea *and* the single most likely thing in this plan to lose you money if you do it the normal way. Both are true. So let's do it the abnormal way.

**The killer isn't creativity — it's cash flow.** Independent magazines don't die from bad writing. They die from printing 3,000 copies, selling 600, and eating the rest.

### The five rules

**1. Annual. Not monthly. Not quarterly.**
One issue a year, maybe two once you know what you're doing. A monthly print magazine will consume your life and your money and produce a worse product. An annual can be *extraordinary* — and an annual is an event, which is much easier to sell than a subscription.

**2. Pre-sell every copy. Print zero on speculation.**
Open pre-orders for six weeks. Print exactly what sold, plus a small buffer. Your inventory risk goes to nearly zero, and your customers fund the print run. This single decision is the difference between a business and an expensive hobby.

**3. Short-run digital printing.**
Digital printing is economical from about 250 copies and the per-copy cost stays fairly flat whether you print 300 or 800. Only move to offset above roughly 1,500 copies, which is a year-three problem. Don't let a printer talk you into a big run for a "better unit price" — that's how the inventory graveyard gets filled.

**4. Price it like an object, not a periodical.**
This is a thing made of paper that a woman who loves stationery and cashmere will keep on a shelf. Indie magazines routinely sell in the $20–35 range. Price at the top of that. Cheap paper and a low price will make it feel disposable, which defeats the entire point.

**5. Hire a freelance art director for one issue. Don't intern.**
You asked whether interning at a magazine makes sense. Honestly — no. It's slow, it's often unpaid, and you'd spend a year learning a workflow built for an economic model that doesn't apply to you. Hire someone good for one issue, sit beside them the whole time, and you'll learn more in six weeks and *own the output.* Trade cook: they need portfolio work, you need a designer.

### Illustrative math

Assumptions, not promises. Verify every number with actual printer quotes before you commit.

**300 copies, ~64 pages, quality stock**

| | |
|---|---|
| Printing (~$8/copy at short run) | –$2,400 |
| Freelance art director (one issue) | –$1,500 |
| Shipping supplies + postage | –$900 |
| **Total cost** | **–$4,800** |
| Cover price $32 × 300 pre-sold | +$9,600 |
| 4 ad pages × $600 | +$2,400 |
| **Revenue** | **+$12,000** |
| **Net** | **≈ +$7,200** |

Not life-changing. But it's profitable, it's low-risk because it's pre-sold, and it produces a physical object that makes every other conversation you have — with brands, with investors, with guests — dramatically easier. **A magazine is the most credibility per dollar you can buy.**

And the ad pages are the real unlock: a brand that won't pay a small creator for an Instagram post *will* pay for a page in a beautiful printed thing, because print still carries prestige pricing that digital reach doesn't.

## PART 3 — THE INSIGHT PLAY (your actual long-term money)

Here's the thing I want you to hold onto.

Every interview you run, you're asking women across five decades what they want, what they're afraid of, what they buy, what they've given up on, and what they'd pay for. **Write it all down. Tag it. Keep it forever.**

**Year one:** don't try to sell this. You don't have the credibility yet and cold-selling research to brands is a grind. Instead, use it as your **sponsorship differentiator.** When you pitch a brand, you're not saying "I have 4,000 followers." You're saying:

> "I've interviewed 40 women aged 18 to 78 about how they actually decide what to spend money on. Here are three things that came up repeatedly that I think your team would want to see. I'd like to talk about a partnership."

That email gets opened. That email gets answered. Nobody else is sending it.

**Year two–three:** package it. A short annual report — *What Women Actually Want: 100 conversations* — sold to brands, or given free to sponsors as the reason to renew. Qualitative depth is the one thing the big panel companies structurally cannot produce, because you can't get a stranger to be honest in a survey checkbox.

**And it feeds the app.** Every system, every want, every complaint becomes product direction. You'd be the only consumer app founder in your category building from a transcript archive instead of a hunch.

## PART 4 — THE MONEY, STAGED HONESTLY

**Months 1–3 — under $500/mo, probably**
Affiliate links start trickling. First ticketed dinners at $9–15 a seat. Substack free only — do not open paid yet, you have nothing to paywall.
*Goal isn't money. It's proof: does anyone show up.*

**Months 4–8 — $500–1,500/mo if it's working**
Substack paid tier opens (paywall the templates and systems, never the essays). Podcast has enough episodes to approach local sponsors. Dinners are weekly and sponsored by local brands. Affiliate compounds.

**Months 9–12 — $1,500–4,000/mo, wide error bars**
Magazine pre-sale lands as a lump. First real brand partnerships using the insight pitch. App beta with early subscribers.

**Year 2 — genuinely uncertain, $4,000–10,000/mo if things go right**
App subscriptions become the base. Second magazine issue, bigger. National sponsors instead of local. Possibly the first insight report.

**What I'd tell you not to count on:** ad revenue inside the app (the math doesn't work at your scale, we went through this), viral growth, and anything happening on the schedule you want it to. Build the plan so that being six months late doesn't kill you.

**What I'd tell you to count on:** the dinners. They're small, unglamorous, and they'll be paying you before anything else does.

## PART 6 — THE RISKS, SAID PLAINLY

**You're building four businesses at once.** Media, events, publishing, software. That's a lot for a team, let alone one person. The sequence above is designed so each one is mostly finished before the next starts. If you compress it, the app will be the thing that suffers, because it's the only one with no immediate feedback loop.

**The magazine will take three times longer than you think.** They always do. Build the schedule with a two-month buffer and don't announce a date until the files are at the printer.

**Print costs are volatile.** Paper, energy, and shipping have all been unstable. Get fresh quotes; don't plan off a number you read once.

**And the one I'd actually watch:** you told me you don't finish things when the environment can't taste the win. This plan has a lot of long middles. Build the reward in on purpose — the dinners are that, actually. They're the only part where you get to see somebody's face when it works.
